GB10 Hard Power-Off under Sustained GPU Load, and the Clock-Cap Thermal Governor

2026-11-07#nvidia#gb10#dgx-spark#gpu#thermal#linux #issue-11

Both GB10 machines here cut their own power while a local model was busy. dgx is an HP ZGX Nano G1n AI Station. dgx2 is an NVIDIA DGX Spark. On dgx the last look at nvidia-smi showed about 77 °C and about 90 W on the GPU, and then the machine was off. There was no warning on screen.

This is a follow-up to the 2026-11-05 note. That note locked the graphics clock at 884–2418 MHz. 2418 MHz is the stock application clock, not a reduction, so the lock does not move the failure point. The lever that does is a ceiling near 2200 MHz, held there by a small governor that watches the ACPI thermal zones.

The journal shows a power cut, not a shutdown

Across 14 boots the unclean ones do not look like an operating-system event.

An unclean boot ends mid-line, on ordinary application output. On several of them the last line is Bitwarden emitting its usual zbus warning. There is no shutdown sequence, no kernel panic, no thermal_sys critical trip, no out-of-memory kill, and nothing in pstore. On the boot before last, that Bitwarden line at 14:25 is the end of the journal. The machine stops having power.

A clean boot looks different, and those are not this failure. logind records “power key pressed”, the GNOME shutdown dialog runs, and systemd powers off in order. Someone pressed the button.

The ACPI critical trip on these boards is 104.8 °C (thermal_zone*/trip_point_*_temp). The kernel never logged reaching it. Whatever opened the power path did it without going through the OS thermal shutdown.

nvidia-smi temperature is the wrong thermometer

On GB10, nvidia-smi reads 8–15 °C below the ACPI zones. A tool that still says the high 70s can be looking at a board that is about to cut power. The governor does not use it.

Read /sys/class/thermal/thermal_zone*/temp and take the hottest zone. The values are millidegrees. A cool zone must not be allowed to hide a hot one, so the controller uses the maximum, not an average.

There is no power limit to set

nvidia-smi -q -d POWER reports Current Power Limit: N/A, and the minimum and maximum are N/A as well. nvidia-smi -pl has nothing to set. A target such as 2000 W is not a meaningful knob here: the adapter is a 240 W USB-C supply, and the GPU was already near 90 W when the power went.

The lever that exists is the graphics clock ceiling, nvidia-smi -lgc. With the stock boost near 2418 MHz these boards pull about 90 W under sustained load and cut power. A ceiling near 2200 MHz keeps the peak near 74 W in the instrumented comparison below.

A static 2200 MHz cap, measured on another GB10

The same cut is already reported for DGX Spark and GB10 boxes from HP, Dell, ASUS, and MSI. One thread is titled for this signature: a hard power-off under sustained GPU load at about 90 W, still there after a full platform firmware update. HP’s security-alerts page for the ZGX Nano G1n also covers unexpected shutdowns under heavy load.

The comparison with a thermometer outside the machine is the ASUS Ascent GX10 writeup. Same class of board, one workflow, four runs in a day. Uncapped, the clock sat near 2450 MHz and throttled itself as low as 1475 MHz. The warm run died at 156 s. Peak board temperature was 96.0 °C, peak power 93.2 W, and 12% of that run was at or above 95 °C. The last sample that made it off the box was acpitz 92.2 °C while nvidia-smi still said 84 °C.

Capped with nvidia-smi -lgc 300,2200, the same warm run finished in 251 s. The clock stayed in the 2164–2184 MHz bin the whole time. Peak board temperature was 91.1 °C, time at or above 95 °C was zero, and peak power was 74.0 W. Against the uncapped cold run in that session (226 s versus 247 s) the wall-clock cost was about 9%. That cost belongs to a native video job that sits on the ceiling for minutes. It is not a measurement of the models on dgx and dgx2.

The useful part of that curve is the throttling. The chip already dropped to 1475 MHz, well under 2200, and the board still cut power. Dynamic slowdown arrives after the heat is in the board. A static ceiling never lets the clock go there, so the heat is not built up in the first place.

fwupdmgr on this HP unit reports EC 3.5.11 and a current UEFI, with no update offered. That matches the forum report in which a full platform firmware update left the cut in place. Firmware reports elsewhere are not uniform, so this is not a claim that an update never helps. On these two units there is nothing newer to install.

The governor that is running on both machines

Both units run the same thermal governor. Every 2 seconds it reads the hottest ACPI zone and moves the graphics-clock ceiling in 100 MHz steps.

The target is 70 °C. The deadband is 3 °C above that and 4 °C below it. At 73 °C or hotter the ceiling steps down. At 66 °C or cooler it steps up. From 67 °C to 72 °C it holds. The floor is 1200 MHz. The hard ceiling is 2200 MHz. At 85 °C it drops straight to the floor instead of stepping. A missing sensor does not raise the clock.

The minimum of the lock stays 300 MHz, so the GPU can still idle. Only the ceiling moves. The service is gb10-thermal-governor.service. It starts after nvidia-persistenced, restarts if it exits, and on stop it leaves nvidia-smi -lgc 300,2200 in place. It never calls nvidia-smi -rgc. A dead governor therefore cannot hand the boost clock back. Stopping the service relaxes a lower ceiling back to 2200 MHz, which is still the safe cap, not the stock 2418 MHz boost.

#!/bin/bash
# GB10 thermal governor. No power limit exists on this part.
# nvidia-smi temperature reads low; control on the hottest ACPI zone.
# Reassert the lock every sample. Never reset clocks on the way out.

LOCK_MIN=300
TARGET=70
STEP_DOWN_AT=73
STEP_UP_AT=66
STEP=100
FLOOR=1200
CEILING=2200
PANIC=85
INTERVAL=2

CAP=$CEILING
SMI=/usr/bin/nvidia-smi

hottest_c() {
  local max=0 seen=0 t f
  for f in /sys/class/thermal/thermal_zone*/temp; do
    [ -r "$f" ] || continue
    t=$(cat "$f" 2>/dev/null) || continue
    case $t in
      ''|*[!0-9]*) continue ;;
    esac
    seen=1
    if [ "$t" -gt "$max" ]; then
      max=$t
    fi
  done
  if [ "$seen" -eq 0 ]; then
    echo na
  else
    echo $((max / 1000))
  fi
}

apply() {
  "$SMI" -lgc "${LOCK_MIN},${CAP}" >/dev/null || true
}

leave_safe_cap() {
  "$SMI" -lgc "${LOCK_MIN},${CEILING}" >/dev/null || true
}

trap 'leave_safe_cap; exit 0' TERM INT

apply
while true; do
  zone=$(hottest_c)
  if [ "$zone" = "na" ]; then
    :
  elif [ "$zone" -ge "$PANIC" ]; then
    CAP=$FLOOR
  elif [ "$zone" -ge "$STEP_DOWN_AT" ] && [ "$CAP" -gt "$FLOOR" ]; then
    CAP=$((CAP - STEP))
    if [ "$CAP" -lt "$FLOOR" ]; then
      CAP=$FLOOR
    fi
  elif [ "$zone" -le "$STEP_UP_AT" ] && [ "$CAP" -lt "$CEILING" ]; then
    CAP=$((CAP + STEP))
    if [ "$CAP" -gt "$CEILING" ]; then
      CAP=$CEILING
    fi
  fi
  apply
  printf 'cap=%sMHz zone=%sC\n' "$CAP" "$zone"
  sleep "$INTERVAL"
done

[Unit]
Description=GB10 thermal governor (ACPI zone, graphics clock ceiling)
After=nvidia-persistenced.service
Wants=nvidia-persistenced.service

[Service]
Type=simple
ExecStart=/usr/local/sbin/gb10-thermal-governor.sh
ExecStop=/usr/bin/nvidia-smi -lgc 300,2200
Restart=always
RestartSec=2

[Install]
WantedBy=multi-user.target

Install the script as /usr/local/sbin/gb10-thermal-governor.sh, put the unit in /etc/systemd/system/gb10-thermal-governor.service, then:

sudo systemctl daemon-reload
sudo systemctl enable --now gb10-thermal-governor.service

The first hours on dgx2

Under real load the ceiling moved with the zone, and it did not sit pegged at the floor:

cap=2000MHz zone=73C
cap=2100MHz zone=64C
cap=2200MHz zone=64C

73 °C was hot enough to step down. 64 °C had cooled enough to step back up, twice, until the hard ceiling. That is the whole mechanism: a few degrees around 70 °C, 100 MHz at a time, never above 2200 MHz.

How to see that the cap took

nvidia-smi -q -d CLOCK still prints Applications Clocks and Max Clocks at 2418 and 3003 after the lock. Those fields do not move. They are the application-clock setting and the design maximum, not the ceiling you just set. Reading them and concluding the lock failed is a known trap.

Under load, check the current clock instead:

nvidia-smi --query-gpu=clocks.current.sm,power.draw --format=csv

clocks.current.sm should stay at or under the ceiling the governor last logged. The query form of -q -d CLOCK shows the same current clock while a job is running.

What this does not settle

The cap is a way around the cut, not a repair. The defect is still in the board. These two machines have not been timed for tokens per second before and after the cap, so the 9% figure stays with the video run it was measured on. Decode that is bound by memory bandwidth should give up less than a job that sits on the clock ceiling; that still wants a measurement on this workload.

Lowering concurrency, which the earlier note did, remains a reasonable way to shorten unbroken full-load stretches. It was not the mechanism. The journal has no software culprit, the OS trip point was never reached, and the clock ceiling is the knob the platform actually exposes.
